These two papers don't share a domain, one is loss-function theory for image classification, the other a speech translation architecture, but they share an approach: both improve an existing pipeline by decomposing a component normally treated as a black box into pieces that can be reasoned about and fixed individually, rather than by scaling up or redesigning the model. The KL loss paper breaks a widely used loss into two algebraically separable terms and finds real problems hiding inside. CRAF takes the same close look at how speech features get compressed and fused, and finds the fix is a pair of targeted modules rather than a redesign.

## Decoupled Kullback-Leibler Divergence Loss

Jiequan Cui, Zhuotao Tian, Zhisheng Zhong, Xiaojuan Qi, Bei Yu, Hanwang Zhang. NeurIPS 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13948

KL divergence loss shows up everywhere in deep learning, adversarial training, knowledge distillation, consistency regularization, but this paper argues it's usually used without much scrutiny of why it works or where it falls short. The authors prove that for softmax classifiers, KL loss is mathematically equivalent to a Decoupled KL (DKL) loss made of two separate pieces, a weighted mean squared error term over the logits and a cross-entropy term with soft labels, just an algebraic rearrangement, not an approximation. Writing it this way exposes two hidden problems. First, gradient flow through ordinary KL loss is asymmetric between the two things being compared, so in settings like knowledge distillation, where the teacher's output is detached from backpropagation, the weighted-MSE half of the loss contributes nothing and useful gradient signal is silently lost. Second, that weighted-MSE term is driven by each individual sample's prediction, so a single badly misclassified example can distort training more than it should. Their fix, Improved KL (IKL) loss, breaks the asymmetry so both halves stay active regardless of which input is detached, and replaces the sample-specific weighting with a class-wise average computed across all samples in that class, which is more stable than any single prediction. Tested on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet, IKL sets a new state of the art on the RobustBench adversarial robustness leaderboard, for example 32.53% AutoAttack robustness on CIFAR-100 with no synthetic training data, beating the prior best by 0.68 points while using less compute, and extends that lead further once diffusion-generated training data is added. On knowledge distillation the result is more incremental, matching or slightly beating prior state-of-the-art methods across most teacher-student pairs tested, while using notably less compute than one strong recent baseline. What makes this worth reading is less the specific numbers and more that the whole fix follows from a clean piece of algebra rather than a new architecture, it's a drop-in replacement anywhere KL loss already sits.

## CRAF: Cross-Modal Representation Alignment and Fusion for Speech Translation

Zhenbei Guo, Wenzhou Wu, Hua Lai, Yan Xiang, Yuxin Huang, Zhengtao Yu. Proceedings of the 24th China National Conference on Computational Linguistics (CCL 2025), pp. 1031-1042. https://aclanthology.org/2025.ccl-1.78/

End-to-end speech translation converts speech directly into another language's text without an intermediate transcription step, but speech representations run much longer than the matching text, so models have to compress them before feeding both modalities into a shared encoder, and prior methods tend to lose real information during that compression and then fuse the two modalities without any signal for which parts actually matter. CRAF targets both problems with two modules added on top of a pretrained HuBERT speech encoder. Its Dynamic Weighted Length-Adapter still compresses the speech sequence through two convolutional layers, but instead of only keeping the final layer's output, it cross-attends the second layer's features against the first layer's and learns a per-position weight that adaptively blends the shallow and cross-attended features rather than discarding the shallow layer outright. Its Representation Fusion Module first aligns the speech and text sequences with optimal transport rather than naive length matching, applies Mixup to blend the aligned representations, then runs self-attention over the mixed sequence so the model can weight the more informative positions instead of treating the fused sequence uniformly. Training follows a pretrain-then-finetune recipe, pretraining on text-only translation pairs, then jointly finetuning on speech-to-text and text-to-text translation together with a KL consistency term pulling the speech, text, and fused representations toward matching distributions. Across eight language pairs from the MuST-C benchmark, CRAF reaches an average BLEU of 29.0, about 0.3 points above the strongest comparison methods, with the largest single-pair gain reaching 0.5 BLEU. An ablation on the English-German pair shows each module contributes on its own, roughly 27.2 BLEU with only the length adapter and 27.3 with only the fusion module, with the full combination doing best. The overall improvement is modest rather than dramatic, which fits the paper's framing, it's fixing two specific failure modes in an already fairly mature pipeline rather than proposing a new paradigm.
