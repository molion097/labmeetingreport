# Lab Meeting - September 10, 2026

This set circles around one mathematical object, the Hilbert space, used for different purposes each time. The pre-image paper is the odd one out, a survey of how to invert kernel feature maps rather than propose a new embedding. The domain adaptation paper learns a Hilbert space where source and target distributions align. The two semantic Hilbert space papers borrow quantum probability formalism to build a Hilbert space for meaning itself, first for text alone, then extended to text-guided image retrieval. Read together it's a good look at how differently people have used the same underlying geometry.

## Preimage Problem in Kernel-Based Machine Learning

Paul Honeine and Cedric Richard. IEEE Signal Processing Magazine, vol. 28, no. 2, 2011, pp. 77-88. DOI: 10.1109/MSP.2010.939747

Kernel methods implicitly map data into a high-dimensional feature space so a kernel function stands in for an inner product there, letting linear techniques like PCA or ridge regression behave nonlinearly without ever computing the mapping explicitly. The catch is that this mapping generally cannot be inverted, so the output of an operation like denoising or a kernel PCA projection usually doesn't correspond to any real input. The preimage problem is the search for an input whose image best approximates such a feature-space point, framed here as minimizing a kernel-based distance objective, essentially a dimensionality-reduction problem in disguise. The paper surveys the main solution families: closed-form inversion for the rare invertible kernels; gradient descent, simple but prone to local minima; the fixed-point iteration scheme popular for Gaussian kernels, easy but numerically unstable and slow; learning-based methods that fit an explicit feature-to-input map from training pairs; and distance-preserving methods rooted in multidimensional scaling, including the authors' own conformal-map approach, which gives a closed-form, kernel-independent solution. On MNIST digit denoising the conformal method gave the cleanest reconstructions in about 1.5 seconds versus 5 minutes for MDS and roughly an hour for 10,000 fixed-point iterations, and the same machinery matched a maximum-likelihood baseline on sensor self-localization from RSSI measurements. It reads as a clear, well-organized reference point for reconstructing input-space signals from kernel-space representations rather than a paper built around one single new result.

## Learning an Invariant Hilbert Space for Domain Adaptation

Samitha Herath, Mehrtash Harandi, and Fatih Porikli. CVPR 2017. https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.08350

Most domain adaptation methods pick a subspace first, usually via PCA on one domain, and only afterward work out how to transfer knowledge into it, which the authors call an arbitrary, unjustified split. This paper learns the space and the transfer jointly instead. Source and target samples are each mapped by their own projection into a shared latent Hilbert space, with a Mahalanobis metric learned in that space at the same time, combining a discriminative large-margin loss over labeled pairs with an unsupervised term that matches the means and covariances of the projected source and target distributions via the Stein divergence between covariance matrices. Because the projections must stay orthogonal and the metric must stay positive definite, the whole thing is optimized as one problem over the product of the corresponding Riemannian manifolds rather than plain projected gradient descent, which the authors found unstable in practice. Classification at test time is just nearest neighbor in the learned space. On the Office+Caltech10 benchmark across Amazon, Webcam, DSLR and Caltech, their method gets the best score on most of the twelve domain pairs for both SURF and deep VGG-FC6 features, for example 95.5 percent versus 90.7 percent for the prior best on one pairing, and it also improves as pose difference grows on the PIE multiview face dataset. It's a clean example of folding representation learning and distribution alignment into one geometric optimization instead of two disjoint steps, though the Riemannian machinery adds real optimization overhead compared to simpler alignment methods.

## Semantic Hilbert Space for Text Representation Learning

Benyou Wang, Qiuchi Li, Massimo Melucci, Dawei Song. The World Wide Web Conference (WWW) 2019. https://doi.org/10.1145/3308558.3313516

The starting complaint here is that most text models compose word vectors linearly, but a lot of meaning is not linear at all, "ivory tower" is not just "ivory" plus "tower". The authors borrow the formalism of quantum probability theory to build a Semantic Hilbert Space, a single complex-valued vector space where sememes, words, sentences and a new learned abstraction all coexist. Each word is a unit-length complex vector over a basis of sememes (minimal meaning units), treated like a quantum particle in a superposition state, with amplitude and phase encoding different layers of meaning such as co-occurrence or sentiment. A sentence, taken as a bag of words, is represented not by summing but as a density matrix, a weighted mixture of the words' superposition states, whose complex off-diagonal entries capture interference between words, exactly where the non-linear composition comes from. A set of trainable measurement projectors is applied to this density matrix via the Born rule to pull out task-relevant probabilities feeding a final classifier, wired together end to end as what the authors call the Quantum Probability Driven Network. On six standard text classification benchmarks covering sentiment, subjectivity and question type, the network matches or edges out CNN and BiLSTM baselines while staying more interpretable, since each layer corresponds to a specific quantum-probability operation rather than an opaque kernel or gate. It's quantum-inspired math, not quantum computing, and the interpretability story is more compelling than the accuracy gains, which are real but modest against strong neural baselines.

## Semantic Hilbert Space for Interactive Image Retrieval

Amit Kumar Jaiswal, Haiming Liu, and Ingo Frommholz. ICTIR 2021. https://doi.org/10.1145/3471158.3472253

Jaiswal, Liu, and Frommholz tackle composed image retrieval, where a user supplies an image together with a short text describing how the target should differ from it, and the system returns matching images from a large collection. The work builds directly on the semantic Hilbert space framework from Wang, Li, Melucci, and Song's WWW 2019 paper above, extending it from single-modality text representation to a genuinely multimodal setting. Text is encoded with BERT and images with a pretrained ResNet-34, both projected into a shared complex-valued Hilbert space rather than concatenated, since raw text and image features have different statistical properties. The central mechanism is a projective transformation that maps the query image toward the target image, with the rotation angle set by the text features, plus a symmetry constraint requiring the complex conjugate of that same transformation to map the target back toward the query, which the authors argue regularizes training and gives text more influence than prior composed-retrieval models where image features tend to dominate. A complex-valued convolutional encoder-decoder learns and reconstructs this joint representation, trained with triplet or softmax ranking losses depending on the dataset. On the MIT-States and Fashion200k benchmarks the resulting model, called SHS, outperforms TIRG and other composed-retrieval baselines on most Recall@K settings, and ablations show the complex-valued space, the feedback branch, and the image-mapping component all contribute. It reads as a competent port of the earlier text-only quantum-inspired geometry into vision-language retrieval, though the "interactive" framing describes a single composed query rather than genuine multi-round feedback, and the gains over TIRG on MIT-States are modest.
