## Are VLMs Ready for Autonomous Driving? An Empirical Study from the Reliability, Data, and Metric Perspectives

Shaoyuan Xie, Lingdong Kong, Yuhao Dong, Chonghao Sima, Wenwei Zhang, Qi Alfred Chen, Ziwei Liu, Liang Pan. ICCV 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04003

This paper asks whether vision-language models can be trusted to explain driving decisions using actual visual evidence rather than just producing plausible-sounding text. The authors build DriveBench, a benchmark of roughly 19,200 frames and over 20,000 question-answer pairs covering perception, prediction, planning, and behavior tasks, tested across 17 conditions spanning clean images, fifteen types of visual corruption such as fog, rain, sensor noise, motion blur, and frame loss, and a text-only setting with no image at all. Evaluating twelve popular VLMs, both open source and commercial, they find a striking pattern, many models perform nearly as well with no image as with a clean one, because they fall back on generic driving knowledge and cues buried in the question text instead of grounding answers in what they actually see. This fabrication is largely invisible to current evaluation practice, since existing driving benchmarks are heavily skewed toward simple majority answers like going straight ahead, and standard metrics such as ROUGE, BLEU, and CIDEr, and even GPT-based scoring, reward fluent text over genuine visual correctness. The models also tend to acknowledge corrupted input only when explicitly prompted, rather than flagging degraded conditions on their own. The paper argues for better-balanced datasets and evaluation protocols that specifically penalize ungrounded answers, and sketches early ideas for exploiting a model's latent corruption awareness to improve reliability. It is a sobering counterpoint to the current enthusiasm for VLM-based driving explanations, showing that confident, fluent language output can mask a real failure to look at the road.


## LaDeL: Lane Detection via Multimodal Large Language Model with Visual Instruction Tuning

Yun Zhang, Xin Cheng, Zhou Zhou, Jingmei Zhou, Tong Yang. Journal of Visual Communication and Image Representation 2026. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvcir.2025.104704

Lane detection underpins visual navigation for autonomous driving, but occlusions and missing lane markers still trip up conventional detectors built around regression or segmentation heads. LaDeL takes a different route, framing lane detection as a task for a multimodal large language model, presented as the first such framework for this problem, so the model reasons jointly over visual and textual input rather than just localizing lines. The key mechanism is a set of lane-specific tokens that let the language model emit precise numerical lane coordinates instead of loose free-form text, paired with an instruction-tuning corpus built by converting standard lane detection datasets into a visual question answering format. That corpus is extended with auxiliary tasks, lane counting and scene caption generation, so the model also learns to describe a scene rather than only mark lines within it, closer to the glance-based scene comprehension people use when driving. On the CULane and TuSimple benchmarks the method reports state-of-the-art results, an F1 score of 82.35 percent on CULane and 98.23 percent on TuSimple, ahead of prior detectors cited in the paper. I could not get past the publisher paywall for the full text, so this summary rests on the abstract and indexed excerpts rather than the complete method and ablations. Even so, it is a clean example of folding a classic perception task into an instruction-tuned vision-language model instead of a specialized architecture, and the open question is whether that reasoning overhead earns its keep against lighter dedicated detectors at similar accuracy.


## GlareLane: A Real-World Benchmark and Method for Lane Detection under Challenging Illumination

Xiaojie Yu, Qiankun Li, Hao Li, Ben-Guo He, Qiang He, Junxin Chen. Image and Vision Computing 2026. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imavis.2026.106141

This one looks at a failure mode that most lane detection benchmarks quietly skip over, glare and abrupt illumination change, sun flare on the windshield, oncoming headlights at night, wet-road reflections, and the sudden dark-to-light swing when a car exits a tunnel. Existing datasets like TuSimple and CULane are dominated by clean daytime driving, so models tuned on them tend to lose lane markings exactly when a driver would most want assistance. The authors address this by building GlareLane, described as the first benchmark collected specifically around these real-world glare conditions across sun glare, night illumination, rain reflections, and tunnel transitions, and pair it with a detection method evaluated against standard baselines including CLRNet. I could not get past the publisher paywall for the full methods and results, so this summary is built from the paper's own descriptive snippets, the journal's indexed abstract text, and the authors' project repository rather than the complete text, and I am deliberately leaving out accuracy numbers I could not verify firsthand. What is confirmed is that the dataset and configuration files are already public on GitHub, with the trained-code release still pending. The value here is less about a clever new architecture and more about naming and packaging a gap the field has been informally aware of, it is the kind of stress test worth running our own lane or road-boundary models against, and I plan to chase down full-text access to check the actual failure rates.


## LINEA: Fast and Accurate Line Detection Using Scalable Transformers

Sebastian Janampa and Marios Pattichis. ICIP 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16264

This one steps back from lanes to the more basic problem underneath them, detecting line segments in an image at all, which feeds camera calibration, SLAM, scene understanding, and low-level perception for self-driving. Transformer detectors like LETR and DT-LSD had already beaten CNN methods on accuracy, but at a cost, both need pretraining their attention mechanism on huge datasets like COCO or Objects365 and run too slowly for real-time use. LINEA's fix is a new attention mechanism called deformable line attention, built on the idea that lines, unlike boxes, have no area, so sampling points should sit directly along the segment between its two endpoints rather than scattered over a region. Paired with a hierarchical backbone, a GELAN-based feature fusion module, and D-FINE style detection heads, this lets LINEA train only on the modest Wireframe dataset with a CIFAR10-pretrained backbone, skipping large-scale pretraining entirely. The result is a family of four models, LINEA-N/S/M/L, running at roughly 2.5 to 5.8ms latency, tens of times faster than LETR and DT-LSD, with far fewer parameters. Accuracy on the Wireframe validation set is competitive but not always best, yet on out-of-distribution testing against YorkUrban, LINEA-L beats every prior CNN and transformer method on every metric. It pairs well with the lane-specific papers today, a reminder that a smarter attention design tailored to the geometry of the target, rather than sheer scale, is what closes the speed-accuracy gap that has held transformer-based line and lane detectors back from deployment.
