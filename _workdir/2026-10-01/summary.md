# Lab Meeting - October 1, 2026

This set is about getting machines to understand everyday sound, from the low-level job to the language-level one. The location-aware HAR paper is the practical edge case, adapting a small sound model to each room on the device. GAMA and Audio Flamingo 2 are the general-purpose audio-language backbones, and the second largely answers the first with better data and cross-attention. EchoScriptor and the soundscape captioning paper then use language models to turn audio into something a person can read, a day's narrative in one case and how a place feels in the other.

## Location-Aware Ambient Sound Model Adaptation for On-Device Human Activity Recognition in Living Spaces

Cheolhwan Lee, Homin Kang, Soon Ju Kang. IEEE Access 2025. https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2025.3568828

Sound-based activity recognition suits homes because it is non-intrusive, but a model trained on a pre-collected dataset degrades when installed in a new room, since the acoustics and the relevant sounds change from place to place. This paper proposes an adaptive ambient sound system for edge devices with three parts. The classification labels can be customized per installation, so each home only uses the activity classes that make sense there. A pre-trained sound model is fine-tuned on the device with locally collected data, a transfer learning step meant to absorb the domain shift. A pseudo-labeling loop then keeps refining the model by retraining on its own high-confidence predictions for unlabeled audio, so long-term adaptation needs little manual annotation. The authors implemented the system and tested it on real recordings from several residential environments. I could only retrieve the abstract, so this summary relies on it alone and has no specific accuracy or latency numbers. It reports only that the system adapted across locations while keeping accuracy high, inference real-time, and training time short. The on-device design is the practical appeal, but the numbers and baselines need checking in the paper.

## GAMA: A Large Audio-Language Model with Advanced Audio Understanding and Complex Reasoning Abilities

Ghosh et al., EMNLP 2024. https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.361/ (arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11768)

Earlier large audio-language models such as LTU and SALMONN bolt an audio encoder onto an LLM through a simple linear connector, which limits alignment and does poorly on questions that need multi-step reasoning about a sound scene. GAMA feeds the LLM three kinds of audio input at once: a custom Audio Q-Former for semantic content, AST features with a multi-layer aggregator that pools low-level and high-level layers, and AST event tags given as a text soft prompt. It is first fine-tuned on more than 2.5M audio-caption pairs, with LLM-written caption rewrites for augmentation, following the LTU recipe with only LoRA trained on the LLM. It is then instruction-tuned on CompA-R, a GPT-generated set of about 200K reasoning question-answer pairs built from audio metadata, plus a 1,561-item human-verified test set. GAMA beats the baselines on classification and captioning by 1% to 84%, and GAMA-IT leads on open-ended and reasoning questions by 8% to 58% on CompA-R-test, judged by GPT-4 and humans. The Q-Former ablation hurts most. The reasoning data is synthetic and GPT-judged, so scores should be read with caution, but the connector design is a useful template.

## Audio Flamingo 2: An Audio-Language Model with Long-Audio Understanding and Expert Reasoning Abilities

Ghosh et al. (NVIDIA and University of Maryland), ICML 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03983

Audio-language models do fine on classification and captioning but stay weak on expert-level reasoning about sounds and music, and almost none of them handle audio beyond about 30 seconds. Audio Flamingo 2 argues the fix is better data and a better audio representation rather than a bigger model. It pairs a 203M audio encoder (AF-CLAP, a CLAP model retrained on over 8M audio-caption pairs with an extra contrastive term for caption paraphrases) with a 3B decoder LM, joined by gated cross-attention. The authors build AudioSkills, a synthetic QA set targeting seven reasoning skills, and train in three stages, ending with a long-audio stage on their LongAudio set of 260k QA pairs for clips up to 5 minutes. They also release LongAudioBench, with 2,429 expert-reviewed items. AF2 beats Gemini Flash v2 on MMAU sound (65.1 vs 61.7) and music (72.9 vs 56.5), and on LongAudioBench scores 64.2 against 45.3. Retrained on the same data, the 7B GAMA reaches only 52.8 MMAU average versus 69.0 for AF2, which suggests cross-attention and the audio encoder matter. The gains lean on synthetic LLM-written QA, so the evaluation is only as reliable as that pipeline.

## EchoScriptor: Automatic Lifelogging Narratives via Activity-Based Audio-Language Model

Kaylee Yaxuan Li, Xinghao Zhou, Haizhong Zheng, Kang G. Shin, and Alanson P. Sample. CHI 2026. https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791528

Camera-free lifelogging is attractive for memory rehabilitation, personal informatics and assistive use, but most audio-based systems stop at isolated event labels, which gives little context and no narrative thread across a day. This paper pairs an activity-based audio-language model with language reasoning so that raw in-home audio becomes a coherent written account of what happened and what the acoustic surroundings were like. EchoScriptor is described as an end-to-end pipeline that goes from recorded sound to context-aware natural-language descriptions and then to a narrative lifelog. I could not get the full text, since the publisher page blocked automated download, so this write-up rests on the abstract and I cannot say much about the architecture or training data. At the moment level it reports 94.15% activity recognition accuracy and 89.25% background recognition accuracy. At the summary level it reaches an F1 of 0.92 and beats a baseline that runs a sound classifier followed by an LLM. In a user study with 20 participants watching 10 household activity videos, its summaries were rated highly and came close to the perceived usefulness of human-written ones. My take is that moving from event detection to narrative is the right direction, though I would want to see results on long real recordings and a discussion of privacy for always-on home audio.

## Soundscape Captioning Using Sound Affective Quality Network and Large Language Model

Yuanbo Hou, Qiaoqiao Ren, Andrew Mitchell, Wenwu Wang, Jian Kang, Tony Belpaeme, Dick Botteldooren. IEEE Transactions on Multimedia 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.05914

Audio scene and event classifiers say what is in a recording but not how it feels to a listener, which is the point of soundscape research. The authors define an affective soundscape captioning task and build SoundSCaper for it. Its acoustic model, SoundAQnet, is a small multi-scale CNN with a graph fusion layer (2.7M parameters) that jointly predicts the acoustic scene, 15 audio event classes and the eight perceived affective qualities, trained on ARAUS, roughly 25,000 clips rated by 605 participants. These outputs go to an off-the-shelf GPT-3.5-Turbo through prompt engineering and token management rather than fine-tuning, which is meant to curb hallucination. SoundAQnet reaches 95.31% scene accuracy on the test set. Two juries of 16 experts and 16 laypersons rated captions on 60 clips. Experts scored SoundSCaper slightly below two soundscape experts, not significantly, and laypersons found them comparable (3.24 versus 3.20 on the external set). It also beats audio captioning baselines such as GAMA and Qwen-Audio, for example CIDEr 0.2745 against 0.1213. The label set is small, so it misses sounds like sirens, and cultural subjectivity stays open. Still, a lightweight acoustic model feeding a generic LLM is a practical design.
