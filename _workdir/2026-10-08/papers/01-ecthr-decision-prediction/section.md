## Using Machine Learning to Predict Decisions of the European Court of Human Rights

Masha Medvedeva, Michel Vols, Martijn Wieling. Artificial Intelligence and Law 28, 2020 (online 2019). https://doi.org/10.1007/s10506-019-09255-y

Courts now publish their judgements, so this paper asks how far plain text classification can predict ECtHR outcomes, and it is a reaction to Aletras et al. 2016, whose 79% result the authors could not reproduce and which used the Law section that often states the verdict. The authors scrape HUDOC, build balanced violation versus non-violation sets for nine Convention articles, and train a separate linear SVM on tf-idf n-grams per article, removing the Law and Judgement sections and keeping only Procedure and Facts, which exist before a ruling. Random-split accuracy averages 75%, from 52% for Article 10 to 82% for Article 2. When models train on cases up to 2013 and predict later ones, accuracy falls to 66-68%, and to 58% with a ten-year gap, so temporal drift is real. A model fed only the surnames of the judges in the Chamber still reaches 65%, which says as much about who sits on the bench as about the text. I like the leakage-aware setup, the temporal test and the open data. The weak points are the simple bag-of-words features, the speculative error analysis, and balancing that hides the fact that the Court mostly finds violations.
