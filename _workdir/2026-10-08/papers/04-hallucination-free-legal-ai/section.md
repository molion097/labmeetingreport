## Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools

Varun Magesh, Faiz Surani, Matthew Dahl, Mirac Suzgun, Christopher D. Manning, Daniel E. Ho. Journal of Empirical Legal Studies 2025 (arXiv 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362

Legal vendors have marketed retrieval-augmented generation as a way to eliminate hallucinations, and this paper from Stanford RegLab and HAI tests that claim. The authors build a preregistered set of over 200 open-ended legal research queries, covering general research and bar-style questions, jurisdiction or time-specific questions, false-premise questions, and factual recall. They run them on Lexis+ AI, Westlaw AI-Assisted Research, Ask Practical Law AI, and GPT-4 as a baseline, and hand-label each answer along two axes, whether it is correct and whether it is grounded in the sources it cites. A hallucination is an answer that is incorrect or misgrounded, while refusals and ungrounded answers count as incomplete. Hallucination rates were about 17% for Lexis+ AI, 33% for Westlaw, 17% for Practical Law, and 43% for GPT-4. Lexis+ AI was the most accurate at 65%, and Practical Law gave incomplete answers more than 60% of the time. Westlaw writes the longest answers, which gives it more claims that can be wrong. Errors included misread holdings and failing to correct a false premise. The take is that RAG helps but does not solve the problem, and the correct/grounded split is a useful way to define hallucination. Evaluation was manual and a snapshot of products that keep changing.
