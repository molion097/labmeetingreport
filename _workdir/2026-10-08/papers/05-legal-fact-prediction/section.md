## Legal Fact Prediction: The Missing Piece in Legal Judgment Prediction

Junkai Liu, Yujie Tong, Hui Huang, Bowen Zheng, Yiran Hu, Peicheng Wu, Chuan Xiao, Makoto Onizuka, Muyun Yang, Shuyuan Zheng. EMNLP 2025 (Main). https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07055

Most legal judgment prediction work feeds the model the legal facts, meaning the facts the judge has already established, but those are only finalized when the ruling is made, so litigants cannot use such a system early. This paper proposes legal fact prediction (LFP): given the plaintiff's claims and the list of submitted evidence, predict the legal facts first, then predict the judgment from them. To test this they build LFPBench, with 657 first-instance Chinese civil cases across ten causes of action. Evidence and claims were extracted with GPT-4o and checked by legal experts, and each claim is labeled fully supported, partially supported or rejected. Six LLMs were compared. For the general-purpose models, predicting directly from evidence averages 46.72% accuracy, against 50.46% with ground-truth facts. Adding LFP lifts it to 48.16%, closing 38.5% of that gap. Better predicted facts also gave better judgments. Open-source legal LLMs were near random, and cases with defendant counterevidence, partial wins and losses were hardest. The models were also biased toward whichever party's evidence came last. My take: the framing matches real practice well, but the data comes from published judgments, so the evidence is cleaner than what litigants really hold, and absolute accuracy stays low.
