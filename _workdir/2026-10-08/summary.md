# Lab Meeting - October 8, 2026

These all sit around one question: how far language technology can be trusted in legal work. The ECtHR paper and PILOT are the outcome-prediction line, and PILOT answers the first one's temporal-drift problem with time-aware retrieval of precedent. Legal fact prediction asks what such systems should actually be given as input. LeCaRDv2 is the retrieval benchmark underneath the precedent-finding step, and the hallucination study is the reality check on the commercial tools that claim to solve all of it.

## Using Machine Learning to Predict Decisions of the European Court of Human Rights

Masha Medvedeva, Michel Vols, Martijn Wieling. Artificial Intelligence and Law 28, 2020 (online 2019). https://doi.org/10.1007/s10506-019-09255-y

Courts now publish their judgements, so this paper asks how far plain text classification can predict ECtHR outcomes, and it is a reaction to Aletras et al. 2016, whose 79% result the authors could not reproduce and which used the Law section that often states the verdict. The authors scrape HUDOC, build balanced violation versus non-violation sets for nine Convention articles, and train a separate linear SVM on tf-idf n-grams per article, removing the Law and Judgement sections and keeping only Procedure and Facts, which exist before a ruling. Random-split accuracy averages 75%, from 52% for Article 10 to 82% for Article 2. When models train on cases up to 2013 and predict later ones, accuracy falls to 66-68%, and to 58% with a ten-year gap, so temporal drift is real. A model fed only the surnames of the judges in the Chamber still reaches 65%, which says as much about who sits on the bench as about the text. I like the leakage-aware setup, the temporal test and the open data. The weak points are the simple bag-of-words features, the speculative error analysis, and balancing that hides the fact that the Court mostly finds violations.

## PILOT: Legal Case Outcome Prediction with Case Law

Lang Cao, Zifeng Wang, Cao Xiao, Jimeng Sun. NAACL 2024 (Long Papers). https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15770

Most legal outcome prediction work targets civil law, where a case is matched against statutes. In case law systems, judges lean on precedent, and the way courts interpret principles drifts over time, so this paper builds a model around those two facts. PILOT has three parts. A retriever is trained with unsupervised contrastive learning (dropout-based InfoNCE) on case text, and it scores candidates by cosine similarity with a time decay, never returning cases later than the target. The labels and similarity scores of the top-k precedents are pooled into an evidence vector and concatenated with a Legal-BERT embedding of the case. A drift module then maps the timestamp through a small MLP and adds the result to the logits. The authors also release ECHR2023, a chronologically split set of European Court of Human Rights cases (8,138 train, 3,000 dev, 3,000 test) summarized with gpt-3.5-turbo. Predicting violated articles, PILOT reaches 0.715 micro-F1, ahead of LWDROV2 at 0.694 and BERT at 0.675, while a 5-shot ChatGPT baseline with retrieved precedents scores only 0.442. Ablations suggest each module adds roughly one point. My take is that the time-aware retrieval is the useful idea, though the gains are modest and ECHR is a narrow testbed.

## LeCaRDv2: A Large-Scale Chinese Legal Case Retrieval Dataset

Haitao Li, Yunqiu Shao, Yueyue Wu, Qingyao Ai, Yixiao Ma, Yiqun Liu. SIGIR 2024 (arXiv preprint 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17609

Chinese legal case retrieval has been held back by small datasets, relevance labels that only look at fact similarity, and candidate pools built from a few lexical models. LeCaRDv1 had just 107 queries and 10,700 candidates over 20 charges. LeCaRDv2 draws 800 queries and 55,192 annotated candidates from about 4.3 million criminal judgments, covering 50 charges. Queries are balanced by charge and by difficulty, using entropy of a judgment-prediction model, plus 100 controversial and 100 procedural queries. Candidates come from a two-level pool. First, BM25, a dense legal RoBERTa and a law-article similarity score (inverse provision frequency) each give 100 cases. Then runs from CAIL2021 narrow these to about 30 promising cases. Legal experts label overall relevance plus characterization, penalty and procedure relevance, following Supreme People's Court guidance. Baselines are evaluated with recall on the full corpus. Lexical models are strong, reaching roughly 0.72 to 0.74 recall@1000 zero-shot, while generic Chinese BERT variants are far worse. Fine-tuned retrieval-oriented models (SEED, RetroMAE) reach about 0.81 to 0.82 at R@1000, yet BM25 still wins at R@100. My take is that this is a useful, carefully built benchmark, though annotation agreement and the heavy criminal-only scope deserve a question or two.

## Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools

Varun Magesh, Faiz Surani, Matthew Dahl, Mirac Suzgun, Christopher D. Manning, Daniel E. Ho. Journal of Empirical Legal Studies 2025 (arXiv 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362

Legal vendors have marketed retrieval-augmented generation as a way to eliminate hallucinations, and this paper from Stanford RegLab and HAI tests that claim. The authors build a preregistered set of over 200 open-ended legal research queries, covering general research and bar-style questions, jurisdiction or time-specific questions, false-premise questions, and factual recall. They run them on Lexis+ AI, Westlaw AI-Assisted Research, Ask Practical Law AI, and GPT-4 as a baseline, and hand-label each answer along two axes, whether it is correct and whether it is grounded in the sources it cites. A hallucination is an answer that is incorrect or misgrounded, while refusals and ungrounded answers count as incomplete. Hallucination rates were about 17% for Lexis+ AI, 33% for Westlaw, 17% for Practical Law, and 43% for GPT-4. Lexis+ AI was the most accurate at 65%, and Practical Law gave incomplete answers more than 60% of the time. Westlaw writes the longest answers, which gives it more claims that can be wrong. Errors included misread holdings and failing to correct a false premise. The take is that RAG helps but does not solve the problem, and the correct/grounded split is a useful way to define hallucination. Evaluation was manual and a snapshot of products that keep changing.

## Legal Fact Prediction: The Missing Piece in Legal Judgment Prediction

Junkai Liu, Yujie Tong, Hui Huang, Bowen Zheng, Yiran Hu, Peicheng Wu, Chuan Xiao, Makoto Onizuka, Muyun Yang, Shuyuan Zheng. EMNLP 2025 (Main). https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07055

Most legal judgment prediction work feeds the model the legal facts, meaning the facts the judge has already established, but those are only finalized when the ruling is made, so litigants cannot use such a system early. This paper proposes legal fact prediction (LFP): given the plaintiff's claims and the list of submitted evidence, predict the legal facts first, then predict the judgment from them. To test this they build LFPBench, with 657 first-instance Chinese civil cases across ten causes of action. Evidence and claims were extracted with GPT-4o and checked by legal experts, and each claim is labeled fully supported, partially supported or rejected. Six LLMs were compared. For the general-purpose models, predicting directly from evidence averages 46.72% accuracy, against 50.46% with ground-truth facts. Adding LFP lifts it to 48.16%, closing 38.5% of that gap. Better predicted facts also gave better judgments. Open-source legal LLMs were near random, and cases with defendant counterevidence, partial wins and losses were hardest. The models were also biased toward whichever party's evidence came last. My take: the framing matches real practice well, but the data comes from published judgments, so the evidence is cleaner than what litigants really hold, and absolute accuracy stays low.
